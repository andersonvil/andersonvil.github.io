## One budget, several people

Household surveys often record income and expenditure for the household as a whole. That is useful, but a total is not the same thing as a distribution. Two households with the same income per person can generate different living standards if they face different needs, share goods differently, or allocate resources differently among adults and children.

Begin with a simple fictional example. Two households each have a weekly budget of 900 units and three members. Dividing by three gives 300 units per person. The arithmetic is correct, but the interpretation is incomplete. Some goods are shared, some are individual, and some members may influence decisions more than others. Housing can be jointly consumed. Clothing is more readily assigned to one person. Childcare uses both money and time. A single average cannot show these distinctions.

## Economies of scale

People living together can share certain costs. A dwelling, heating, and some durable goods do not need to be tripled when a household grows from one member to three. This is an economy of scale. It means that simple per-capita income can understate the resources available to members of larger households.

Equivalence scales address this issue by converting household resources into a comparable measure. They are useful, but they embed assumptions about needs and sharing. A fixed scale should therefore be understood as a measurement convention, not as a direct observation of individual welfare.

## Resource shares

Suppose the shared-cost problem is solved. We still do not know how the remaining resources are distributed. A household total can be consistent with equal sharing, unequal sharing, or different allocations across goods. Researchers may use assignable goods, behavioral restrictions, or collective models to learn about individual resource shares.

The distinction matters for poverty measurement. A household classified above a poverty threshold may still contain a member with limited access to resources. Conversely, a household with modest cash income may benefit from shared goods in ways that a per-capita calculation misses. Neither possibility can be resolved by totals alone.

## Time belongs in the conversation

Living standards also depend on time. Care, commuting, market work, and household production compete for a finite day. A family may purchase a service or provide it with unpaid time. Looking only at cash expenditure can miss this substitution.

Time should not automatically be valued at a person's wage. That choice may be convenient, but it assumes that the relevant hour is interchangeable with market work. In some activities, time has both a productive component and direct value to the person performing it. A careful model states which concept of time value it uses and why.

## A classroom exercise

Give students three fictional households with identical income but different rent, care time, and assigned spending. Ask them to calculate:

1. income per person;
2. disposable resources after housing;
3. a simple equivalence-scale adjustment;
4. the share assigned to each member under two alternative allocation rules;
5. a written explanation of what remains unobserved.

The final item is the most important. Economic measurement improves when students can say both what a statistic captures and what it leaves unresolved.

## From average to evidence

Household averages are a starting point, not a verdict. Better analysis combines transparent definitions, explicit assumptions, information on individual goods or time use, and sensitivity checks. The goal is not to reject aggregate data. It is to avoid asking an aggregate measure to answer an individual question without additional evidence.
